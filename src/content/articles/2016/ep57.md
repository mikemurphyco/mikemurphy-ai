## The Basics at the Recording Phase

**Tip:** keep iPhones and iPads away from speakers and the recording interface — they can introduce RF interference and static that's easy to miss live but maddening on playback.

### Mono vs. Stereo

**Mono** means a single sound channel — left and right speakers play identical audio. If recording from one microphone, mono is all that's needed. **Stereo** has separate left/right channels, useful when capturing multiple sound sources (a band, for instance). Unless a podcast leans heavily on music or effects — like an audio drama — mono is the right export choice in nearly every case. **File settings used here:** mono, 44.1kHz, 32-bit float.

### Gain, Levels & dB

**Gain** is the input level set on a mixer or interface. **Levels** are the bouncing meter lights (green/yellow/red) shown while recording or playing back — mostly green, with louder moments hitting yellow, is the target. Red means **clipping** or **peaking**, which is bad and should always be avoided. **Decibels (dB)** measure where those levels sit — 0dB is where clipping begins, so anything positive is unusable. **Tip:** aim for input levels between -18dB and -9dB to leave headroom — the cushion between -9dB and 0dB that gives room to edit and add effects. Finished audio might land closer to -3 or -1.5dB, but headroom during recording makes that possible.

**Audition keyboard shortcuts:** Option+I to view levels, Shift+Spacebar to start recording.

### Waveforms

The mountain-range-shaped green lines seen in any audio editor — a visual representation of the audio itself, useful for reading loudness and dynamic range at a glance.
